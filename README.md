# Assessing the Potential of Imaging Spectroscopy to Detect Agricultural Microplastics in Soils with Varying Clay Composition
<img align = "left" src="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/sarp-patch.jpeg?w=1280&format=webp" alt="drawing" width="200"/> Over the past 8 weeks I have had the most amazing experience interning for the [NASA Student Airborne Research Program (SARP)](https://science.nasa.gov/earth-science/early-career-opportunities/student-airborne-research-program/). I was apart of the Terestrial Ecology West cohort, where my faculty mentor was Dr. Dan Sousa of San Diego State University, and my graduate student mentor was Samantha Madonia of San Diego State University. My project focused on assessing the ability of NASA's Earth science instruments to detect microplastics in agricultural soils. 

## Abstract
Modern agricultural plastics, such as mulch films and synthetic fibers, can enhance productivity but they can eventually degrade into microscopic particles, which can harm soil health and hinder plant growth. The effect of these agricultural microplastics on soil optical properties, and thus their detectability in airborne or spaceborne platforms, remains poorly understood. Here I evaluate whether laboratory and airborne spectroscopy can be used to distinguish between soils with microplastic concentrations ranging from 0 to 1% across different clay compositions. Reflectance spectra of prepared soils with known microplastic concentrations and clay compositions were measured in the laboratory using a Spectra Vista Corporation HR-1024i spectrometer. Laboratory soil samples were prepared from various mixtures of sand, silt, organic carbon content, and either kaolinite or bentonite clay. Microplastic treatments included 1% polyethylene mulch film, 1% polypropylene flat fragment, or 1% polypropylene fiber, and a plastic-free control. To explore image-scale detectability, AVIRIS imagery over agricultural fields was analyzed using spectral angle mapping (SAM) and matched filtering (MF) based on laboratory-derived spectral signatures. Across laboratory samples, microplastic addition reduced the depth of the absorption feature at 2200 nm by an average of 2.85% relative to plastic-free controls. The band depth at 2200 nm of soils containing bentonite decreased by 5.15% in the presence of microplastics. Polyethylene mulch microplastics were the most absorptive of the microplastics tested, with an average decrease in band depth of 5.61% at a concentration of 1%. Analysis of AVIRIS and EMIT images filtered using SAM and MF suggests the ability to map agricultural microplastics in fields with a similar soil type and the ability to distinguish between clay types. Together, these results suggest that agricultural microplastics can measurably alter soil reflectance spectra in some circumstances, supporting future use of NASA airborne and spaceborne imaging spectroscopy to assess microplastic contamination in exposed agricultural soils.
